Choose and scope your first AI workflow, in one working session.

What this is, and what it is not

This is a fill-in workbook. You work through five steps and finish with a one-page Workflow Brief: a clear, bounded description of one workflow that you could hand to a teammate, a vendor, or an AI tool and get something useful back.

It will help you decide which workflow to start with and what "done" means. It does not promise savings, revenue, accuracy or adoption. Every number you write down here is a hypothesis you will check, not a fact.

Time needed: 60 to 90 minutes. You need: this document, a copy you can edit, and one person who knows how the work is done today (that may be you).

Step 1. List candidate workflows (10 minutes)

A workflow is a repeatable piece of work with a start, an end and an owner. "Customer support" is a department. "Draft a first reply to a refund request" is a workflow.

List five to eight. Don't judge yet.

No.Workflow (verb + object)Who does it todayHow often

Prompts to find them: What do you re-type or re-paste every week? What waits in someone's inbox until they have time? What do new hires always ask how to do? Where do you copy information from one place to another?

Step 2. Score each one (15 minutes)

Score each candidate 1 (low) to 5 (high) on the six questions. Be honest; a low score is useful information.

How to read it

  • Pick the highest total, but only if Safe data and Low stakes are both 3 or more. A high-scoring workflow with sensitive data or costly mistakes is not a first workflow. Park it.
  • If the top two are within two points of each other, choose the one with the clearer steps. A clear process is easier to improve than a vague one.
  • If nothing scores 3 or more on Clear steps, your first task is to write down how the work is actually done. That alone is worth doing.

Step 3. Map how it works today (15 minutes)

Write the real steps, including the awkward ones. Do not describe how it is supposed to work.

StepWhat happensWhoTool or placeRough time

Then answer:

  1. Trigger. What starts this workflow? (An email, a form, a date, a request.)
  2. Inputs. What information does the person need in front of them?
  3. Output. What exactly is produced, and where does it go?
  4. Judgment calls. Which steps need a person's judgment, not just effort?
  5. Where does it go wrong today? Late, inconsistent, missing details, rework?

Rules of thumb

  • Anything that reaches a customer, a regulator or a payment is human-reviewed, at least at the start.
  • Final decisions about people (hiring, pay, discipline, eligibility) stay human. Do not use this kit to automate them.
  • "Candidate for automation later" is a place to park ideas, not a plan.

Step 5. Write the one-page Workflow Brief (20 minutes)

Copy this page into its own document and fill it in. Keep it to one page.

Workflow Brief: [name]

Owner: [person accountable] Date: [date] Version: 1

Goal (one sentence). [What should be better, for whom?]

Trigger and inputs. [What starts it, what information is needed.]

Output. [What is produced, in what format, delivered where.]

Current process (3 to 6 steps). [From Step 3.]

Proposed split. Human: [..]. AI drafts, human reviews: [..].

Data rules. May use: [..]. Must never use: [..].

Success measure. [One number you can actually collect.] Baseline today: [measured, or "unknown, measure first"] Target (hypothesis): [a number you will test, not a promise] Measured over: [period]

Acceptance criteria. The work counts as done when: (1) [..], (2) [..], (3) [..].

Known risks. [What could go wrong, and who notices.]

Out of scope. [What this brief deliberately does not cover.]

First 30 days. Week 1: [..]. Week 2: [..]. Week 3: [..]. Week 4: review against the success measure and decide to continue, change or stop.

Check your brief. Someone who has never seen this workflow should be able to read it and say: what is being done, who owns it, what "good" looks like, and what is off limits. If they can't, tighten it.

Illustrative example (hypothetical, not a client case)

This example is invented to show the format. It is not a customer result and the numbers are made up.

Workflow Brief: First-draft replies to routine refund requests

Owner: Support lead Version: 1

Goal. Cut the time a support agent spends writing a first reply to routine refund requests, without changing who approves refunds.

Trigger and inputs. A refund request arrives in the shared inbox. Needed: the order number, the refund policy page, and the customer's message.

Output. A draft reply, saved as a draft in the helpdesk, never sent automatically.

Proposed split. Human: approving or refusing the refund, sending the reply, anything involving a complaint or legal wording. AI drafts, human reviews: the wording of the first reply.

Data rules. May use: the refund policy and the customer's message text. Must never use: payment card details, health information, or other customers' records.

Success measure. Median minutes from request opened to reply sent. Baseline: measure for two weeks first. Target (hypothesis): lower than baseline; set the number after the baseline exists.

Acceptance criteria. (1) Every draft is reviewed by a person before sending. (2) No draft contains a refund promise that the agent did not approve. (3) Agents can edit or discard any draft in one click.

Out of scope. Chargebacks, disputes, and any request mentioning a lawyer or regulator.

Starter prompts (optional)

If you use an AI assistant to help you write the brief, give it your Step 3 notes and the data rules first. Use a tool your organisation has approved, and never paste anything from your "must never use" list.

  1. "Here are my notes on how this workflow works today. Ask me up to ten questions about anything that is unclear before drafting a brief."
  2. "Turn these notes into the Workflow Brief format below. Mark anything you had to assume with [ASSUMPTION] so I can check it."
  3. "Read this brief as a skeptical colleague. List the five most likely ways it fails, and what a person would notice first."
  4. "Suggest three acceptance criteria that a person could check in under a minute each."

Always read the output yourself. An AI tool can sound sure and be wrong.

What to do next

  1. Run your brief for 30 days on a small scale. Review it against your success measure at the end.
  2. Keep what worked, change what didn't, and write Version 2.
  3. Then pick the next workflow and repeat the five steps.
